Once, in a quiet workshop, a curious clockmaker built a little wooden bird.
The bird could not fly, but it listened carefully to every sound in the room.
It heard rain tapping on the window, a kettle singing on the stove, and pages
turning in the old book beside the lamp. Each morning the clockmaker wound the
bird and asked it what it had heard. At first it repeated only a few sounds.
With time, it learned that rain came before the river rose, and that the kettle
whistled when the water was ready. The bird made no grand predictions. It
simply noticed patterns, one small moment after another. One bright morning,
sunlight crossed the workbench, and the bird began a new tune of its own.

Learning a language model is a little like teaching that bird. We show it many
examples, let it find patterns, and ask it to guess what comes next. A guess
can be wrong, so we measure the error and gently adjust the model. Repeating
this process does not give a tiny model human understanding. It gives the model
a chance to become better at predicting the examples it has seen.

In a small library at the edge of town, a blue notebook rested on a desk.
Every afternoon, a student opened it and wrote one observation from the day.
Some notes described clouds gathering above the hills. Others described the
warm smell of bread from the market or the soft crunch of leaves underfoot.
The notebook grew slowly, line by line. When the student reread it, familiar
details began to connect: cloudy mornings often brought rain, and the baker
opened the shop before the first bus arrived. The notes were simple, but they
made a useful record of patterns in a changing world.
